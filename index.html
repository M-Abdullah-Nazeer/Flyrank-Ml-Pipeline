Which visible pages will keep under-capturing clicks? A decision-support ranking on Search Console data

Muhammad Abdullah Nazeer · FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Lane: CTR / engagement opportunity scoring

Abstract

Question. FlyRank’s refresh work flags pages that people see in search but do not click, and rewrites titles when CTR falls below the benchmark for a position tier. A flag does not say which pages matter most, so which already-visible pages will keep earning fewer clicks than expected for their position and should be reviewed first?

Method. From anonymized daily Search Console data, I built page-level features from days 1–15 of a month and a label from days 16 onward, then trained a gradient-boosting ranker on Feb–Mar 2026.

Result. On held-out April 2026, it reached AUC 0.789 against 0.680 for a simple missed-clicks rule, and 92% of its top 500 pages truly under-captured (base rate 50%).

Validation. With a later month and unseen clients, the lead held but narrowed (AUC 0.769 vs 0.679), on a small sample.

Use. I turn the ranking into a human-reviewed action playbook. The results are observed associations for decision support, not proof of cause.

1. Introduction

Content teams cannot review every page. Many pages are visible in search yet earn fewer clicks than pages at the same position usually do, which points to a snippet, intent or content problem worth a look. The decision this work supports is narrow: which pages should a person review first, and what should they check? The output is a ranked queue with a reason code per page, not an automatic editor.

The case study: a CTR flag needs a priority order

FlyRank’s public April 2026 report describes optimization flags such as “Fix CTR” (people see the page but do not click) and a workflow that rewrites titles and descriptions when CTR drops below position-tier benchmarks. A flag shows that a problem may exist, not which flagged pages deserve an editor’s time first, and a flag could simply reflect one noisy week. This paper uses that situation as its case study. It first asks whether the gap is real and persistent: in the warehouse data, pages whose CTR was far below the expected level for their position got about 0.32× the expected clicks later in the month, against 1.6× for pages above it. It then ranks pages by how likely the gap is to persist and how many clicks it affects, so a person can start where review is most likely to matter. It uses my own expected-CTR curve from the warehouse, not FlyRank’s flags or scores, and it draws only on anonymized data and public statements.

2. Data

Release. FlyRank ML Internship pseudonymized warehouse (July 2026 release), table fact_content_daily_performance, Search Console columns only. One row is one page on one day for one client; the grain was verified (March: 9,841,378 rows, 9,841,378 distinct keys).

Windows. Training months 2026-02 and 2026-03; evaluation month 2026-04. Within each month, features use days 1–15 and the label uses day 16 to month end. The final month (2026-06) was kept sealed and not used.

Availability. Rows were kept only where Search Console data was available (IS TRUE): 3,611,061 of 9,841,378 March rows (36.7%).

Excluded and why. GA4, session-channel and AI-referral columns (a different lane); pages with under 100 impressions in days 1–15 or average position beyond 20 (too noisy to judge CTR); product flags and health scores (leakage risk); all identifiers, domains and queries from this page (public safety). 3. Methodology

Label

A page is labelled 1 if its clicks in days 16+ are below 50% of the clicks expected for its position bucket (1–3, 4–6, 7–10, 11–20). Expected CTR is the pooled CTR of each bucket, computed from training months only. The 50% cut-off is my choice, so some “wrong” picks are near misses.

Features (all from days 1–15)

Log impressions, average position (impression-weighted), CTR, CTR gap ratio (CTR divided by expected CTR), impression trend (days 8–15 vs 1–7) and days with data.

Baselines and models

(a) The Week-4 rule: missed clicks = impressions × (expected CTR − actual CTR); (b) CTR gap ratio alone; (c) logistic regression; (d) gradient boosting. The more complex model is preferred only if it wins clearly on held-out data.

Validation design

Main split: train on Feb+Mar, test on Apr, with every method scored on the same test pages. Robustness: random rows (the easy version), clients held out, and April pages of clients never seen in training.

Leakage checks

Features end on day 15 (checked in code). The label window is never used for features. Expected CTR comes from training rows only. A deliberate leak (adding future clicks) raised AUC to 0.985, which confirms the audit would catch one. No single feature predicts the label near-perfectly (strongest: CTR, single-feature AUC 0.75). About 80% of April pages also appeared in training months; on never-seen pages AUC was 0.769 against 0.788 overall.

What the model leans on and where it errs

Permutation importance is dominated by the CTR gap ratio (AUC drop 0.177), well ahead of log impressions (0.044) and CTR (0.015); the other three features each add under 0.006. Among the model’s top 1,000 picks, 8.9% were wrong. Errors concentrate on top-3 pages (13.5% wrong), where a search feature may take the click, and on mid-volume pages with 1k–5k impressions (13.1%). Many false positives were near misses just above the cut-off; false negatives were pages whose CTR collapsed later in the month, which no day 1–15 feature can foresee.

5. Limitations and honest framing

These are observed associations over three months of data, useful for decision support.

  • The ranker shows which pages are likely to keep under-capturing, not why, and not that a rewrite or refresh would raise clicks.
  • The label cut-off (50% of expected) and the expected-CTR curve are my choices; the curve is only roughly monotonic across position buckets.
  • No page age or update date was available, so I could not test a time-since-refresh effect.
  • Only pages with data in both halves of a month are evaluated. Page identities were not matched across months for evaluation.
  • The unseen-client check is one small draw. Clients differ, so results may not carry to new sites.
  • The priority queue (risk × size) had 0.853 precision in its top 500, lower than ranking by model score alone (0.924), because large pages come first.

Decay insight. Pages with a shrinking impression trend under-captured 67% of the time, against 49% for flat and growing pages: a falling trend adds risk, and growth gives no protection. This is directional; it is not a test of refreshing.

Cost and value. Assuming 20 review minutes per page, the top 100 pages offer about 426 missed clicks (days 1–15 proxy) per review hour; the top 1,000 offer 146. Value falls mostly because lower-ranked pages are smaller, so start at the top and stop where extra hours no longer pay.

Human review and what not to automate

Before acting, a person checks for search features, how far below expected the CTR is, sudden position or impression moves, intent fit, and facts and brand voice. Do not automate publishing, page deletion or merging, top-3 edits without the search-feature check, causal promises to clients, or use outside positions 1–20 and 100+ impressions.

Monitoring and retrain triggers (proposals)

Each month, re-check on the newest month. Retrain if precision@500 falls below base rate + 0.15, AUC falls below 0.70, or the label rate shifts by more than 0.10. Rebuild the expected-CTR curve if the population stability index of CTR gap ratio or position exceeds 0.25. April values (0.924, 0.789, −0.018, 0.043, 0.015) sit well inside these limits.
